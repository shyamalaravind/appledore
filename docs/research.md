# The research behind the writing rules

appledore's writing rules ([skills/study/writing-rules.md](../skills/study/writing-rules.md)) were checked against the research on learning from text: cognitive load, expertise reversal, coherence, signaling, worked examples, refutation texts, question generation and the curse of knowledge. This page collects what that check found, item by item, with links to every source, and says plainly where the evidence is thin. The [README](../README.md#why-it-works-this-way-the-research) has the short version.

## How much weight it carries

Most of this research tested students in lab sessions, with short texts and memory tests a few minutes later. Few studies used working adults.

Effect sizes from one lab, Mayer's, run larger than independent estimates. A meta-analysis across Mayer's whole body of work found g = 0.37 over 591 effects ([Cromley & Chen 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2025.100730)).

The review mostly read abstracts. Where a number comes from a secondary source, the item says so.

So treat each finding below as a direction. The rules use the direction, not the number.

## The findings

**1. Working memory holds three to five new things, and what counts as "new" depends on the reader.**
- **The limit.** [Cowan (2001)](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X01003922) and [Cowan (2010)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2864034/) found a central limit of about four chunks (3–5) in young adults, when rehearsal and grouping are blocked. These were list tasks, not reading.
- **What counts as new.** [Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-019-09465-5): working memory is limited for new information, not for information already organised in long-term memory. How many elements interact depends on the content and on what the learner knows.
- **So:** "at most three new concepts" is a sensible margin under that limit, not a number the research gives. An engineer is a novice in a new product's own terms but not in general software ideas, so a note defines the first and assumes the second.

**2. Help a novice needs gets in an expert's way.**
- **The effect.** [Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler & Sweller (2003)](https://doi.org/10.1207/S15326985EP3801_4) named the expertise reversal effect, and showed that expertise is specific to a domain.
- **The meta-analysis.** [Tetzlaff, Simonsmeier, Peters & Brod (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2025.102142) pooled 60 studies and 5,924 people:
  - low-knowledge learners gained from more help (d = 0.51);
  - high-knowledge learners did better with less (d = −0.43);
  - results varied widely between studies.
- **So:** this supports a reminder of a few words over a second definition.
- **Not cited.** No study tested in-text reminders of terms an earlier note taught. "Just-in-time" presentation ([Kester, Kirschner & van Merriënboer 2006](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cedpsych.2005.04.002)) did not confirm its own hypothesis, so it isn't cited as support.

**3. Cut what the question doesn't need.**
- **Seductive details.** Interesting but irrelevant details hurt learning, and the effect holds for text:
  - [Rey (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2012.05.003), 39 effects: leaving them out helped retention (d = 0.30) and transfer (d = 0.48);
  - [Sundararajan & Adesope (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-020-09522-4), 58 studies: including them cost g = −0.33.
  - A 2026 conference meta-analysis, not peer-reviewed, puts the effect at g = −0.16.
- **Practitioner support.** [Diátaxis](https://diataxis.fr/explanation/) warns that an explanation "tends to absorb other things", so reference detail and steps belong elsewhere.

**4. Write the links between ideas in; repair the gaps.**
- **Low-knowledge readers.** [McNamara, Kintsch, Songer & Kintsch (1996)](https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci1401_1): low-knowledge readers did better with a more coherent text. The "reverse cohesion effect" for high-knowledge readers showed only on deep measures.
- **Skilled readers too.** [O'Reilly & McNamara (2007)](https://doi.org/10.1080/01638530709336895): skilled high-knowledge readers also did better with high cohesion.
- **Repairing gaps.** [Britton & Gülgöz (1991)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.83.3.329) repaired each place where a sentence shared nothing with the one before ("inference calls"), and recall improved. A revision aimed at a better readability score did not help.
- **A caution.** [Strohmaier et al. (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2023.100533) is a meta-analysis of 45 experiments (N = 6,477):
  - language changes had a small average effect (g = 0.15), larger for readers with little prior knowledge;
  - clearer, more elaborated text helped;
  - adding cohesion alone had no significant effect.
- **So:** write the link in, with its reason, rather than leaving the reader to infer it.

**5. Give the precise reason, or none.**
- **The experiment.** [Stein & Bransford (1979)](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-5371%2879%2990481-X) measured recall of a sentence's key word:
  - with a precise reason, 74%;
  - with an imprecise one, 22%;
  - with no reason, 42%.
- **The caveat.** The numbers come from a secondary book (Schwartz, Tsang & Blair, 2016), not the paper itself, and the materials were isolated sentences, not connected text.
- **A related rating.** [Dunlosky et al. (2013)](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266) rate elaborative interrogation of moderate use, but that is a strategy the learner uses, not a feature of a text.
- **So:** a vague or invented "because" can do worse than none. That is why the writing rules give the consequence when the sources give no reason.

**6. Signal the structure with headings and a few bold terms, and signal all of it.** A note with no headings until a filed passage adds one produces exactly the partial signaling that Lorch found harmful.
- **Signals in general.** [Schneider, Beege, Nebel & Rey (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2017.11.001), 103 studies:
  - signals improved retention (g = 0.53) and transfer (g = 0.33);
  - structuring signals in text, such as headings and bold words, gave g = 0.71 for retention.
- **Signal all of it, and only the targets.**
  - [Lorch & Lorch (1996a)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.88.1.38): signals helped mainly with complex texts. When only half the topics were signaled, the other half was recalled worse than with no signals at all.
  - [Lorch, Lorch & Klusewitz (1995)](https://doi.org/10.1006/ceps.1995.1003): underlining only the target sentences helped, while underlining many more sentences cancelled the gain.
- **Headings in short texts.**
  - [Lorch & Lorch (1996b)](https://doi.org/10.1006/ceps.1996.0022): headings helped recall of unfamiliar topics.
  - [Prinz-Weiß & König (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.4076): five texts of about 500 words, with a heading before each paragraph, raised comprehension (d = 1.00). This is one small online study (51 students).
  - [Hartley & Trueman (1985)](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00052394): question and statement headings did equally well.
  - [NN/g (2019)](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/text-scanning-patterns-eyetracking/), an industry study: readers who scan from heading to heading do best.
- **Caveats.** The readers were students, and the tests measured memory. The review found no study of what bold costs the unbolded text.

**7. Put each paragraph's point first.**
- **Research.** [Kieras (1980)](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03198274): readers picked out a passage's intended theme more often when its theme sentence came first. The effect was small, and it measures what readers take as the main idea, not what they learn.
- **Practice.** [Google's Technical Writing One](https://developers.google.com/tech-writing/one/paragraphs) says "The opening sentence is the most important sentence of any paragraph". [GOV.UK](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/writing-guidelines/clear-structure/) and [digital.gov](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language) agree.

**8. Short sentences and paragraphs, but no formula.**
- **The guides.** All of these are practitioner guidance, not research:
  - [GOV.UK](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/writing-guidelines/clear-language/): "Try to split up sentences that are over 25 words long", and at most five sentences per paragraph;
  - [Army Regulation 25-50](https://armypubs.army.mil/epubs/DR_pubs/DR_a/ARN42124-AR_25-50-007-WEB-13.pdf): sentences of about 15 words on average;
  - [Google](https://developers.google.com/style/paragraph-structure): short paragraphs.
- **Formulas predict comprehension poorly.**
  - [Klare (1976)](https://doi.org/10.1080/10862967609547171) asked whether readability makes a difference, and answered "Sometimes".
  - [Duffy & Kabance (1982)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.74.5.733): a formula is not an effective writing criterion.
  - [Davison & Kantor (1982)](https://doi.org/10.2307/747483): splitting sentences to hit a formula score sometimes made texts harder.
- **Plain rewrites help real readers.** [Suchan & Colucci (1989)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0893318989002004002): 262 naval officers read a plain-style memo 17–23% faster and understood it better.
- **An unsourced figure.** The often-quoted American Press Institute figures (8-word sentences understood by 100% of readers, 14-word sentences by 90%) have no source anyone can find.
- **So:** a word ceiling is a check, not a target, and no split may drop the "because".

**9. Address the reader as "you".**
- **For it.** [Ginns, Martin & Marsh (2013)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-013-9228-0), a meta-analysis of 3,312 students: conversational style improved retention (d = 0.30) and transfer (d = 0.54), though not in lessons over 35 minutes.
- **Against it.** [Lindhaus et al. (2024)](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1405308) found no gain with written materials.
- **The guides disagree.** [Microsoft](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/style-guide/top-10-tips-style-voice) says "Write like you speak", while [Google](https://developers.google.com/style/tone) warns against writing exactly as you speak.
- **So:** the effect is weak, but writing "you" costs nothing.

**10. Name the parts before showing how they work.**
- **Pre-training.** [Mayer, Mathias & Wetzell (2002)](https://doi.org/10.1037/1076-898X.8.3.147): learning each part's name and states before a narrated animation improved transfer in three experiments.
- **Elements one at a time.** [Pollock, Chandler & Sweller (2002)](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0959-4752%2801%2900016-0): novices learned very complex material better when they met its elements one at a time first.
- **Caveat.** Both used animations or vocational training, and self-paced text may need this less.

**11. One concrete case, then the rule.**
- **Worked examples help novices.** [Atkinson, Derry, Renkl & Wortham (2000)](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543070002181) showed it, and [Barbieri et al. (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-023-09745-1) found g = 0.48 over 55 studies. But nearly all of that evidence is for problem-solving skills, such as algebra. The review found no test on purely conceptual explanation, so this rule rests on analogy.
- **Concrete to abstract is unproven.** Concreteness fading ([Fyfe, McNeil, Son & Goldstone 2014](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-014-9249-3)) recommends moving from the concrete to the abstract. A later review finds it less general than claimed ([Kokkonen & Schalk 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-020-09581-7)).
- **So:** the note states each general rule outright after the case.

**12. Say where a comparison breaks, and name the trap.**
- **Analogies.** They help when the mapping is made explicit ([Gentner 1983](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog0702_3); [Richland & Simms 2015](https://doi.org/10.1002/wcs.1336)). Learners need help to see the differences as well as the similarities.
- **Unstated limits.** The claim that they cause misconceptions rests mostly on case studies and work with children ([Harrison & Treagust 1993](https://doi.org/10.1002/tea.3660301010); [Zook & Di Vesta 1991](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.83.2.246); [Spiro et al. 1989](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511529863.023)).
- **Analogy and literal text do different jobs.** An analogy helped novices on inference questions, while the literal text helped on detail ([Donnelly & McDaniel, 1993 and 2000](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03214368)). That is why the writing rules add the comparison to the plain statement.
- **The trap callout.** Refutation texts state a common wrong belief and correct it. They beat ordinary texts in a meta-analysis of 71 articles and 294 effect sizes, and none of the moderators tested changed that ([Danielson et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2024.2365628)). This is the support for the one warning callout.

**13. A small diagram, next to the words that say the same.**
- **Words plus pictures.** Mayer reports large gains from words plus pictures (median d = 1.35) and from placing them together (d = 0.82), all from one lab. An independent meta-analysis found text plus diagrams the most consistent of the media types (Cromley & Chen 2025, above).
- **Simple beats detailed.** [Butcher (2006)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.98.1.182): simplified diagrams supported factual learning and integration best.
- **Matching words to picture parts.** [Richter, Scheiter & Eitel (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2015.12.003): showing which words match which parts of a picture helped (r = 0.17), especially low-knowledge learners. So a diagram's labels use the note's own words.
- **An Obsidian detail.** A link inside a Mermaid node doesn't appear in the graph view ([Obsidian help](https://help.obsidian.md/advanced-syntax)), so the prose should link the note too.

**14. Put the point first: weak evidence, strong practice.**
- **Practice agrees.** All of these put the main point first:
  - "bottom line up front" in [AR 25-50](https://armypubs.army.mil/epubs/DR_pubs/DR_a/ARN42124-AR_25-50-007-WEB-13.pdf);
  - the [inverted pyramid](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/subject_specific_writing/journalism_and_journalistic_writing/the_inverted_pyramid.html);
  - [Minto's pyramid](https://www.barbaraminto.com/);
  - Microsoft's "Get to the point fast";
  - GOV.UK's "Put the most important information first".
- **Research is thin.**
  - **Prequestions** improve learning of what they ask about ([St. Hilaire, Chan & Ahn 2024](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-023-02353-8): g = 0.54 on the asked content, g = 0.04 on the rest; [Pan & Carpenter 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-023-09814-5)). But the benefit comes from trying to answer before reading. A question answered in the next lines was not tested.
  - **Advance organizers** have a small effect ([Luiten, Ames & Ackerson 1980](https://doi.org/10.3102/00028312017002211): 0.21 on average across 135 studies, 0.17 for written ones).
  - **Curiosity** as an information gap ([Loewenstein 1994](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.116.1.75)) is theory.
- **So:** a note opens with its point in one line, then its case. An opening question would be a cheap signal, not a proven lever.
- **Headings are different.** GOV.UK advises against question headings ([clear structure](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/writing-guidelines/clear-structure/)), so the headings name their parts.

**15. A writer can't see past what they know, so a cold reader checks.**
- **Informed people can't ignore what they know.** [Camerer, Loewenstein & Weber (1989)](https://doi.org/10.1086/261651): informed traders could not ignore what they knew, and market experience cut the bias only by about half.
- **Experts misjudge novices.** [Hinds (1999)](https://doi.org/10.1037/1076-898X.5.2.205): experts predicted novices' task times worse than intermediates did, and resisted debiasing.
- **Instructions don't fix it.** [Damen et al. (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1037/xlm0000830): telling people to take the other person's view did not reduce the bias.
- **The tappers.** In [Newton's 1990 dissertation](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/cognitive-bias/illusion-of-depth/1990-newton.pdf), tappers expected listeners to name 50% of the songs; listeners named 3 of 120. It was never published in a journal, and its own abstract gives a different count.
- **The remedy.** [Pinker (2014)](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/observer/the-curse-of-knowledge-pinker-describes-a-key-cause-of-bad-writing), a practitioner, advises showing the draft to people like the audience. That is the cold reader.

**16. Small, linked notes that grow.**
- **Matuschak's evergreen notes,** practitioner guidance: [atomic](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/zNUaiGAXp21eorsER1Jm9yU) ("only about one thing"), [concept-oriented](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/z2hQEhqWkdRLL9JUwfawZZx), [densely linked](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/zF8xCU4BwXwbmSyp7tmff9i), and [accreting over time](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/zTn3g4wTm1hbkNFUvLLjpev).
- **zettelkasten.de** treats atomicity as "a guiding compass" ([guide](https://zettelkasten.de/atomicity/guide/)).
- **Why reading isn't enough.** ["Why books don't work"](https://andymatuschak.org/books/) argues that reading explanatory prose rarely produces understanding without active reflection, and the read → ask loop is that reflection. His remedy also includes spaced repetition, which this design leaves out.

**17. Asking your own questions.**
- **The finding.** [Rosenshine, Meister & Chapman (1996)](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543066002181): teaching students to generate questions while reading improved comprehension, by a median of 0.36 on standardized tests and 0.86 on the researchers' own tests.
- **The caveat.** The students were trained and prompted, so this doesn't show the effect of an adult's unprompted questions.
- **So:** it supports the ask step, not quizzes.

## Where the evidence is weak

- **The numbers.** None comes from a study: 150–450 words, three new concepts, a 100-word overview, a 60-word filed passage, a 25-word summary. Cowan's three to five chunks and the plain-language guides are the nearest support.
- **The labelled opening lines and the closing section.** These are practice, not research.
- **Bold.** The review found no study of what bold costs the unbolded text.
- **"You".** Its effects are small and inconsistent.
- **The reminder in place of a second definition.** It is inferred from expertise reversal, and untested in this form.
- **Headings in a short note.** They rest on one small study plus Lorch's work on unfamiliar topics. Lorch also found that signals do little in simple texts.
- **Mayer's effect sizes.** They come from one lab, and independent estimates are smaller (g = 0.37 overall).
